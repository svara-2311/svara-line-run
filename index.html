About

Product manager, building through a techno-commercial lens.

Machines came first.

Formula Student taught me how a machine works. Every design decision showed up as a part I could hold, with a weight, a price and a reason to exist. Engineering management taught me what that part costs over its whole life. Now I ask both questions of everything: what am I optimizing for, and who pays for it?

Industrial data science was the way in.

I built a digital twin of a refinery and argued over how its data should travel. I tuned a remaining-useful-life model, where the real decision was what the optimizer should minimize. These were among the first instances where a software decision changed what a machine did, not just what a dashboard said about it.

Robotics is where it led.

At Applied Materials I build robotic systems for semiconductor fabs. I start with what the hardware can actually do and match it to a customer problem worth solving. Then I carry it through specs and pilots, building toward go to market in a Tier-1 fab.

I learn by building.

I build a small version of everything I care about: AI retrieval, evals and memory, a robot in simulation, a venture community. Each one shows me which part is actually hard, and it’s rarely the part I expected.
